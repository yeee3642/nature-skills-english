## Step 1: Define the Research Search Space

Produce a one-page territory broad enough for alternatives yet narrow enough for bounded exploration. Identify the phenomenon or process, distinct meanings, possible actors/materials, setting, time, scale, undecided boundaries, and constraints.

### Required input

Record Mode A/B/C; interest and motivation; possible participants, organisations, or materials; institutional/historical context; time or change process; known concepts and literature; exclusions; language, location, time, budget, access, ethics, and skills constraints; and the largest uncertainty. Motivation does not establish scholarly importance.

### Work

Disentangle core concepts rather than adding synonyms. Offer adjacent vocabulary, explain how boundary choices change scope, expose assumed directions and causal/value judgements, and propose provisional inclusion/exclusion boundaries.

Do not generate PhD questions, titles, hypotheses, novelty claims, or a favoured method. Dataset convenience must not decide scope. Large searches are usually unnecessary; any terminology lookup retains its source level and uncertainty.

### Save

Save the Search Space with alternative terms, boundaries, exclusions, constraints, decisions, and gate. Preserve at least two possible directions. A reader should describe the territory in a minute.

### Gate

`PASS`: concepts are distinct, boundaries usable, constraints and undecided points visible, and exploration remains open.

`REVISE`: scope is too broad/narrow, concepts conflated, or data/method preferences dominate. Revise one decisive boundary rather than generate questions.

`STOP`: interest is not sustained, ethics unacceptable, or fixed language, location, time, or access conditions rule out the direction. Save the reason and revisit broader interests or changed conditions.

If three or more checks remain unanswered, do not advance: clear territory; distinct constructs; actors/materials/context/time/scale boundaries; alternatives; motivation separated from importance; constraints affecting scope; and no premature questions or novelty claims.

### Illustrative correction

An interest in care does not become a research problem by asking how care affects society. Social media and identity may combine governance, self-presentation, belonging, and several identities. Break these apart before generating topics.

## Step 2: Map the Research Landscape

Organise a scholarly conversation by problem families, explanations, mechanisms, debates, and boundaries, rather than one abstract per paper.

### Required input

Carry the Search Space, distinct concepts, alternative terms, actors/materials, time and scale, exclusions, seed papers if any, suspected explanation, constraints, and unknowns. Do not ask only for support for a conclusion.

### Quick Run

Use at least three search-language groups. Identify roughly 3–5 problem families and 2–4 explanations/frameworks; seek at least one contradiction, null, reversal, or alternative explanation; retain at most five sources revealing field structure.

Record what each source supports, DOI/stable link and evidence level, a major boundary, key unknown, and challenge to the starting view. Record date, terms, and omissions. Stop with a Quick Run gate.

Quick `PASS` permits Deep Run when the researcher chooses further investment; it does not replace it. `REVISE` repairs a decisive terminology/evidence gap. `STOP` returns to Step 1 when no conversation emerges or interests and constraints do not fit.

### Deep Run

Use multiple vocabularies and adjacent literatures, with targeted backward/forward citation tracing. Distinguish synonyms from adjacent constructs. Inspect decisive full texts and official source documentation where available.

Map problem families; theories and limits; mechanisms; competing explanations; evidence traditions; methods and limitations; relatively robust findings and bounds; contradictions, nulls, reversals, negative cases; and population/context/time/scale limits.

Label consequential claims `METADATA ONLY`, `ABSTRACT LEVEL`, `FULL-TEXT LEVEL`, or `PRIMARY/OFFICIAL`. Titles and search snippets cannot establish detailed methods/results. Citation counts and journal prestige do not establish evidence strength.

Retain date, databases/materials, query groups, time/language limits, failures, inaccessible key texts, and adjacent fields not covered. No result within a search boundary does not establish absence everywhere.

### Save and gate

Save the Landscape Map, compact key-source table with evidence levels, boundaries and failures, and changes needed to the initial idea.

`PASS`: multiple problem/theory families are visible, counterevidence and limits preserved, and the initial view located in a specific debate.

`REVISE`: the map only confirms the initial idea, depends on insufficient abstracts, conflates concepts, or has unhelpfully narrow/wide scope. Repair the decisive gap without creating an unbounded systematic review.

`STOP`: reasonable searching finds no conversation, or closest work resolves the supposed difficulty without a remaining knowledge problem. Revisit Step 1 and preserve why.

## Step 3: Find Unresolved Research Problems

Extract 3–7 consequential uncertainties before writing final research-question sentences.

A thinly studied population or place is coverage information. Explain which institutional condition, mechanism, measurement, or theoretical expectation could differ and why it changes understanding.

### Four-question test

For each problem ask:

1. What is already known?
2. What exactly remains unexplained, disputed, or inadequately observed?
3. Why would resolving it change understanding?
4. What evidence could distinguish the possibilities?

If any answer reduces to nobody has done this before, do not pass it.

### Problem types

Classify the difficulty as conflicting findings, competing explanations, unclear mechanism, measurement failure, unobserved timing/process, unexplained heterogeneity, scale mismatch, new evidence for an old problem, or scientifically justified literature integration.

Apparent conflicts may instead reflect constructs, populations, time windows, scales, or biases. Identify which interpretation the evidence supports.

A mechanism explains how phenomena are produced, sustained, or changed. Specify actors/conditions, sequence, and traces in observations, narratives, documents, behaviour, or history. A mediator, interview theme, or list of causes is not itself a mechanism.

Missing methods are not contributions. Explain what observation is absent and how a method changes what can be known. Longitudinal data, interviews, or machine learning matter only when serving that task.

### Input and save

Use the landscape, robust findings, debates, contradictions/nulls, measurement/method/time/scale limits, new materials, evidence levels and search bounds, and 1–3 matters the researcher most wants to understand.

For each Problem Card save the four answers, type, closest work and reading depth, strongest alternative and objection, terminating/downgrading evidence, unknowns, researcher motivation, and one next check.

Do not declare novelty. Seek material showing that the problem is resolved or rests on invalid comparisons. Preserve rejected cards and reasons.

### Gate

`PASS`: grounded, consequential uncertainties have distinguishable explanations and plausible observations worth mapping in Step 4.

`REVISE`: problems remain topics/coverage gaps, consequences are circular, closest work insufficiently inspected, or distinguishing evidence unspecified. Revisit concepts or landscape.

`STOP`: close work resolves all uncertainties, apparent conflict disappears after construct correction, or no knowledge consequence survives. Save the record and revisit Search Space.

## Quality checks and handoffs

Red flags: unsupported importance/novelty, premature methods, abstracts instead of a landscape, exclusively supportive sources, equating similar terms with identical constructs, or a method's absence presented as a contribution.

After each gate update Current Research State. Carry the current output, decisive sources and levels, counterevidence, constraints, unknowns, and next check. Keep full chats, search lists, and obsolete alternatives in their original records.
