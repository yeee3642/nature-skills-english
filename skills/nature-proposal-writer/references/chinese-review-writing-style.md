# Academic Review Writing Style

<!-- English edition: modified 2026-10-20; see EDITION-NOTICE.md at package root. -->

Use these evidence-led style rules for review articles. English is the default;
language-specific typography applies only when the user requests another language.

## Section titles and paragraphs

Titles should identify the research object and analytical angle. Avoid slogans,
personification, exaggerated metaphors, and conversational questions. Prefer
"Factors affecting material stability" to "What determines a material's fate",
and "Characterization of interface changes" to "How can we see the interface?"

Give each paragraph one identifiable claim. State it early, then supply evidence,
comparison, and limitations. A short paragraph of roughly three to five sentences
often works better than a long paragraph carrying several claims. Transitions
should express an actual logical relation rather than rely on filler such as
"moreover" or "it is worth noting". Apply indentation in export styles only when
the target format calls for it.

## Precision and claim strength

Replace generic openings with the background fact. Replace "important
significance" with the particular understanding, method, or decision changed.
Qualify "fills a gap" with a verifiable account of search coverage. Define what
"systematic", "comprehensive", or "in-depth" covers. Use sequence words only for
a real sequence.

Report numbers and conditions instead of unsupported intensifiers: for example,
"increased by 28.6%" or ">=0.10 wt%" when those are supported values. Do not use
"significant" to imply a statistical result that was never tested. Keep observed
findings, model predictions, author interpretations, and untested hypotheses
distinct. Use "reported" for results, "proposed" for models or ideas, and "proved"
only when the evidence actually warrants that strength.

State the material, conditions, timescale, and measurement method needed to bound
a comparison. Keep symbols, subscripts, superscripts, units, variable styling,
and reaction notation consistent.

## Remove formulaic writing

Flag sentences that only restate the preceding sentence, claim a broad analysis
without defining its coverage, create an artificial "not only ... but also"
progression, or end with generic claims of practical value. Excessive connectors
can hide a broken argument; varying sentence form cannot repair missing evidence.
Restore the claim, evidence, and limitation before polishing the language.

Use explicit subjects and unambiguous references. Give each sentence one main
judgment and split it when nested clauses obscure that judgment. Prefer clear
active wording when it identifies who measured, modelled, or inferred the result.
Keep causal, contrasting, comparative, and limiting relationships explicit.

## References

Each important factual claim needs a traceable citation or clearly marked citation
placeholder while drafting. Prefer original, representative, methodologically
comparable studies to mechanical citation piles. Check major claims against the
primary research instead of treating a review author's paraphrase as the original
evidence. Do not add causal interpretations that the cited paper did not support.
For conflicting results, compare conditions, methods, and uncertainty rather than
forcing a single conclusion.
